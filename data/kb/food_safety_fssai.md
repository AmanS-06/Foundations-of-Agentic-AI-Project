# Food safety rules for cooked and leftover food (FSSAI catering guide)

Source: FSSAI, "Food Industry Guide to implement GMP/GHP requirements: Catering", first edition, October 2017. Section numbers are the guide's own. Page numbers are the footer numbers printed in the PDF. Temperatures are written as degrees Celsius (C).

## About this source and what it does not say

The rules in this document come from one guide: the FSSAI "Food Industry Guide to implement GMP/GHP requirements: Catering" (first edition, October 2017, front matter and disclaimer). The guide is guidance for food businesses. Its preface says its use is voluntary, and its disclaimer says it does not replace any legal provision. Where the guide conflicts with Part II or Part V of Schedule 4 of the FSSAI licensing and registration regulations of 2011, the regulations prevail. This project cites the guide's section numbers and not clause numbers of the regulations, because we did not check those clauses. The guide has no separate rule that all leftovers must be discarded after a fixed number of hours at room temperature; its time limits are the ones listed in this document. The 2-hour/4-hour rule that is used in some other countries' guidance is not part of this guide. For food kept at normal temperature, the only limit longer than 2 hours in the main text is the transport rule (section 2.8(f), p.31). That rule is about carrying chilled-served food, not about leftovers in a canteen. Sections below that are marked "Project interpretation (ours)" are our reading, not FSSAI text.

## Hot and cold holding temperatures for high-risk food

FSSAI Catering Guide, section 2.2(e), p.24: high-risk foods shall be stored at or below 5 C or at or above 65 C. Section 2.6.2(g), p.30: high-risk food should be displayed at 5 C or below, or at 65 C and above. Section 2.1(k), p.22: high-risk food should be received at 5 C or below. The guide gives meat, fish, poultry and dairy products as examples of high-risk food (section 2.1(m), p.22) and does not give a full list. Hot food on display should be kept in heating containers, in small portions that can be refilled, and the temperature should be checked at regular intervals (section 2.6.2(h), p.30). Cold food should be kept in a refrigerator or on ice, and refrigerators should show their temperature (section 2.6.2(i), p.30).

The range between 5 C and 65 C is where the guide gives time limits instead of a holding rule. See the sections on hot food below 65 C and cold food between 5 C and 10 C.

Ambiguity: the inspection checklist in the Annexure (item on holding temperatures, p.60) asks for hot food at 65 C and for non-vegetarian hot food at 70 C. The main text says 65 C. This project uses one hot-holding figure, 65 C, for every dish.

## Hot food that has fallen below 65 C: the 2-hour rule

FSSAI Catering Guide, section 2.6.2(k), p.30: hot food that is kept at a temperature below 65 C must be consumed within 2 hours. If it is not consumed within 2 hours, it must be reheated to 75 C with a minimum contact time of 2 minutes, and it may then be consumed. The same clause ends by saying that such food shall not be reheated or consumed after 2 hours.

Ambiguity: the last sentence can be read in two ways. It may mean that food reheated after its 2 hours must not be reheated again or kept longer. Or it may mean that food which stayed below 65 C for more than 2 hours must not be eaten at all. The guide does not say which. This project takes the stricter reading for food left at room temperature, as explained in the section "Project interpretation (ours)". The checklist in the Annexure (p.60) asks only whether hot food is held at 65 C.

## Cold food between 5 C and 10 C

FSSAI Catering Guide, section 2.6.2(l), p.30: cold food may be kept above 5 C but below 10 C for up to 2 hours, but this can be done only once. If any of that food is left after this, it shall be discarded. In other words, a cold dish may sit slightly warm on a counter for up to 2 hours one time. After that, the remaining portion of the dish is thrown away.

This is separate from the hot-food rule. The hot-food rule (section 2.6.2(k)) applies to food that was cooked hot and has fallen below 65 C. The cold-food rule applies to food that is meant to be served cold, such as a chilled dish that has warmed up between 5 C and 10 C. The checklist (Annexure D, p.60) repeats the cold-food limit of two hours, only once.

## Reheating cooked food: method and temperature

FSSAI Catering Guide, section 2.5(a), p.29: reheating should be done by direct methods, such as heating on a stove or using a microwave. The food shall be heated uniformly for a contact time of at least 2 minutes. Section 2.5(b), p.29: indirect heating is not acceptable. The guide names a bain marie, adding hot water, and keeping food under a lamp as examples that do not count as reheating. Section 2.6.2(k), p.30: reheating means reaching 75 C, with a minimum contact time of 2 minutes.

The sample HACCP table for reheating in the Annexure (p.57) says to heat food to a core temperature of 75 C or above as quickly as possible, measured with a core food thermometer on each batch. The inspection checklist (p.60) asks that the core temperature reaches 75 C and is kept there for at least 2 minutes. This project therefore requires 75 C at the core of the food for at least 2 minutes, using a stove or a microwave, and it checks each reheated batch with a thermometer.

## Reheating only once: meat, poultry, fish and gravies

FSSAI Catering Guide, section 2.5(c), p.29: high-risk foods such as meat, poultry, fish and gravies should be reheated only once and then consumed. The leftovers after that shall be discarded. So a chicken dish, a paneer curry or any other gravy that has been reheated one time must not be reheated a second time, and whatever is not eaten after the reheat is thrown away.

Ambiguity: the guide says "such as", so the list is not complete, and it does not say whether cooked rice, dal or vegetables count as high-risk food. This project is cautious: it treats every cooked dish as high risk, and it allows at most one reheat for any leftover batch (the batch record keeps a count of reheats). A batch that has already been reheated once is not reheated again. It is discarded.

## Cooling cooked food before refrigeration

FSSAI Catering Guide, section 2.4(f), p.28: cooked or semi-processed high-risk food that is going to be refrigerated shall be cooled from 60 C to 21 C within 2 hours or less. It must then be cooled from 21 C to 5 C within a further 4 h, so the whole cooling takes at most 6 hours. The guide suggests dividing food into smaller portions or shallow containers, cutting large joints of meat and poultry into smaller pieces, and leaving space around containers in the refrigerator so cold air can move.

Ambiguity: the Annexure does not match this section exactly. The sample HACCP cooling table (pp.56-57) uses 63 C and 20 C for the first stage and asks for 4 C within 6 hours in total. The inspection checklist (p.60) says the second stage should be done in two hours or less. This project follows section 2.4(f) of the main text: 60 C to 21 C in 2 hours, then 21 C to 5 C. Food left standing at room temperature has not been cooled this way.

## Eat cooked food within 24 hours of preparation

FSSAI Catering Guide, section 2.4(f), p.28: cooked or semi-processed high-risk food that has been cooled and refrigerated shall be consumed within 24 hours of preparation. The 24 hours are counted from when the food was prepared (cooked), not from when it went into the refrigerator. After 24 hours the food is not served and is discarded.

In the guide this sentence is written for food that was cooled and refrigerated. This project applies the 24-hour limit to every leftover batch, however it was kept, because it is the simplest safe choice (our interpretation). The "shelf life in hours" given for each dish in menu.csv (between 3 and 8 hours) is a separate figure. It is an assumption made for this project, not an FSSAI number, and the 24-hour limit is a different and longer rule that applies on top of it.

## Transporting cooked food

FSSAI Catering Guide, section 2.8(d), p.31: high-risk food shall reach the point of consumption within 2 hours of preparation and should be consumed immediately. Section 2.8(e), p.31: if it is not to be eaten immediately, or is carried for longer, it must be kept at 65 C during transport. Alternatively it must first be chilled to 5 C or below and then carried at 5 C or below. Section 2.8(f), p.31: high-risk food that is meant to be served chilled must be kept at 5 C or below during transport. If it is carried at normal (ambient) temperature, it shall be consumed within 4 hours.

That 4 hours is a transport allowance for chilled-served food carried without cooling. It is not a general time limit for leftovers in a kitchen or on a counter. This project does not use it for decisions about leftover batches. The inspection checklist (Annexure D, p.60) says food is transported hot at 65 C, cold at 5 C, or within 2 hours of preparation.

## Receiving food and using stock in the right order

FSSAI Catering Guide, section 2.1(g), p.22: check the expiry or best-before date, the packaging, the storage condition and the temperature of packaged produce when it is received. Section 2.1(i), p.22: the quantity of food received should match the immediate requirement and the storage space available. Section 2.2(h), p.24: stored raw materials, ingredients, work in progress and finished products shall follow FIFO (first in, first out) and FEFO (first expire, first out). The guide explains FEFO as: the food that will expire first is consumed before food that expires later. Section 2.2(j), p.24: stored food should be tagged with the date and time to support stock rotation and prevent spoilage.

For the canteen this means that when two lots of the same ingredient are in the store, the lot with the earlier expiry date is used first, and received stock is dated when it comes in.

## Project interpretation (ours): may a leftover batch be served again

This section is how our project applies the guide to one leftover batch. It is our interpretation and not FSSAI text. The batch record holds the dish, the time it was cooked, how it is kept (hot, chilled or ambient), its temperature, and how many times it has been reheated. The numbers used are 65 C, 2 hours, 75 C, one reheat and 24 hours.

1. Older than 24 hours since cooking: blocked. It is discarded.
2. Kept hot at 65 C or above and under 24 hours old: may be served.
3. Kept at room temperature, or at any temperature between 5 C and 65 C, for more than 2 hours: blocked. Example: a 6-hour-old paneer curry kept at room temperature may not be served again. The guide's wording on reheating after 2 hours is ambiguous (section 2.6.2(k)). We take the stricter reading, because food held at room temperature for hours has not been cooled as section 2.4(f) requires.
4. Already reheated once, and high risk (we treat every cooked dish as high risk): blocked.
5. Chilled correctly at 5 C or below, under 24 hours old, and never reheated: may be reheated to 75 C for at least 2 minutes and served once. Whatever is left after that is discarded.

If more than one rule applies, the stricter outcome wins. We cannot check the cooling curve from the batch record, so "chilled correctly" is taken from the recorded holding state. A person trained in food safety makes the final decision. This is a planning aid, not a certified food-safety system.
